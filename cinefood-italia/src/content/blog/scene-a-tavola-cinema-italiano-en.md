In Italian cinema food is never just food: it is story, identity, comedy and
nostalgia. Here are some of the dining scenes that left a mark, in
chronological order.

## The ritual of Roman cuisine

*[An American in Rome](/en/film/un-americano-a-roma)* (1954), directed by
**Steno**, gave us one of the most famous scenes ever, with pasta as the
star: Nando Mericoni, after snubbing Italian cuisine in the name of the
American dream, finally gives in to macaroni with the line "Maccarone,
you provoked me and I'll destroy you!" — an ironic hymn to the capital's
working-class cuisine.

## Fellini's high society

*[La Dolce Vita](/en/film/la-dolce-vita)* (1960) made Via Veneto a
byword for glamour and paparazzi, with the real Café de Paris as the true
heart of that era — even though the street scenes were largely rebuilt at
Cinecittà.

## Conviviality as storytelling

From *[Welcome to the South](/en/film/benvenuti-al-sud)*, with its dinners
among colleagues, to the culinary discovery of *[Eat Pray
Love](/en/film/mangia-prega-ama)*: the table becomes the place where
characters truly meet — the film's high point is the scene, among the most
quoted in recent cinema, of **[Julia Roberts eating pizza in
Naples](/en/blog/eat-pray-love-pizza-da-michele-napoli)**.

## Elegance and nostalgia

The refined dinners of *[The Great Beauty](/en/film/la-grande-bellezza)*
(2013), Sorrentino's Oscar winner, and the timeless cafés tell of an Italy
suspended between beauty and melancholy.

## Food as investigation: Montalbano

Even television has its own ritual at the table: Inspector
*[Montalbano](/en/film/il-commissario-montalbano)*, based on Andrea
Camilleri's novels, never solves a case on an empty stomach. His solitary
lunches of fresh fish, often eaten in silence facing the sea at Punta
Secca, have become as iconic as the investigations themselves — another
way Italian screens turn food into a pause, a thought, a pleasure.
