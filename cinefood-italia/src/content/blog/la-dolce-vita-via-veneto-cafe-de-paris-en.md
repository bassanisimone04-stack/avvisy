No film has portrayed high-society Rome like Federico Fellini's *La Dolce Vita*
(1960). The heart of that world of paparazzi, film stars and jet set was
**Via Veneto**, and one of its real-world symbols was the
**[Café de Paris](/en/locale/cafe-de-paris-roma)**.

## The Café de Paris, the real hangout that inspired the film

Opened in 1948, the Café de Paris became the emblem of the real *dolce vita*
of the late 1950s: outdoor tables, Italian and international stars, and
photographers lying in wait to catch them. It's in this atmosphere that a
word entered the vocabulary of the entire world: **"paparazzo"**, the name
of the film's photographer character (inspired by a character in George
Gissing's book *By the Ionian Sea*), today a universal synonym for
celebrity photographer.

## A detail few people know: the film's Via Veneto isn't the real one

A surprising detail: the Via Veneto scenes in *La Dolce Vita* **were not
filmed on the real street**, but on a faithful reconstruction Fellini had
built on **Studio 5 at Cinecittà**. The director wanted full control over
lighting, crowds and framing — impossible on the real street, already busy
even then. Fellini himself said he found his Cinecittà Via Veneto "more
beautiful and more true" than the real one. This doesn't take anything away
from the real Café de Paris's role: it was there, on the actual street,
that the paparazzi phenomenon and the "dolce vita" itself were born, well
before becoming a film set.

> Note: the Café de Paris **closed in 2008**, after a judicial seizure.
> There has long been talk of a redevelopment project (Rome's city council
> has announced investment in the street), but for now it remains shut.

## Other historic cafés on Via Veneto

The Café de Paris wasn't alone: the same street was also home to **Harry's
Bar** (still open today, at number 150) and the famous **Doney**, another
regular haunt of Fellini himself with his wife Giulietta Masina, and of
Luchino Visconti. Visitors to Via Veneto today can still sit down at
Harry's Bar for a drink on the very stretch that was the heart of high
society Rome.

## What to see today along Via Veneto

Strolling along Via Vittorio Veneto means walking through the Rome that
inspired the film: the grand historic hotels like the Excelsior, the cafés,
the steep climb up to Porta Pinciana and the gardens of Villa Borghese. A
perfect itinerary for lovers of auteur cinema, a short walk from Piazza
Barberini and the Spanish Steps.

You will find the venue page **[Café de Paris](/en/locale/cafe-de-paris-roma)** and
the film's stops on **[La Dolce Vita](/en/film/la-dolce-vita)**, along with all the
venues of **[Rome](/en/roma)**.

## In short

*La Dolce Vita* is Via Veneto, even though the film's Via Veneto is (in
part) a masterpiece of set design built at Cinecittà: the real beating
heart of that era, the **[Café de Paris](/en/locale/cafe-de-paris-roma)**,
now closed, remains the actual place where it all began — walking along
Fellini's street is still, today, a small tribute to the most glamorous
Rome in film history.
