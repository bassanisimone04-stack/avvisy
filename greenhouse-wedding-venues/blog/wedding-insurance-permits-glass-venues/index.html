Wedding insurance and permits for greenhouse and garden venues

The paperwork side of booking a glasshouse or botanical garden venue: what coverage venues ask for, who is responsible for it, and how permits fit in.

By GreenhouseVenues editorial

Published 2026-09-18 · Updated 2026-09-18

In short

  • Most public gardens and estate venues require a one-day liability policy naming the venue as additional insured, separate from any coverage a caterer already carries.
  • $1 million per occurrence / $2 million aggregate is a common baseline, though the specific venue sets its own minimum.
  • A public garden or municipal glasshouse may also require a separate use permit, with its own lead time.
  • Basic coverage is inexpensive and quick to buy — the risk is forgetting to ask until a venue requests proof close to the date.

What "event insurance" actually means here

A one-day special event liability policy is a short-term insurance certificate that covers a single date, protecting the policyholder (usually the couple, sometimes a vendor) against claims like a guest injury or property damage during the event. It is separate from a caterer’s or florist’s own business liability insurance, which covers their operations generally, not your specific wedding date.

Public gardens, museums and historic estates ask for it more consistently than a standard rented hall, largely because the building or grounds they are protecting are harder and more expensive to replace.

How much coverage venues typically ask for

$1 million per occurrence and $2 million aggregate is a common figure for outdoor and public-venue weddings in the US, and it is what most one-day policies default to. Some city- or county-run gardens set a higher minimum. The venue’s own rental packet is the source that matters here — a generic figure from a blog post, including this one, is a starting point for the conversation, not a substitute for asking.

Cost and timing

Basic one-day liability policies commonly run well under $200, with higher coverage limits or weather-cancellation add-ons costing more. Most insurers let you buy coverage right up until shortly before the event, which makes it easy to postpone — buy it once your date and headcount are confirmed rather than leaving it for the final week, since some venues want the certificate on file in advance, not on the wedding day itself.

Permits are a separate question from insurance

At public botanical gardens and municipal glasshouses, a use or event permit is often required on top of the rental fee and the insurance certificate, with its own application and sometimes a longer lead time than either. See our botanical garden rules guide for what else differs about renting inside a public institution.

Who is responsible: the couple or the vendors?

It genuinely varies. Some venues only require the couple’s own policy; others require every vendor on site — caterer, florist, band or DJ — to carry and prove their own coverage as well. Get the specific requirement in writing from the venue rather than assuming one party’s insurance covers the whole event; see our vendor logistics guide for what caterers and florists specifically need to know.

Quick answers

Insurance and permits FAQs

What kind of insurance do greenhouse and garden wedding venues actually require?

Most commonly a one-day special event liability policy that names the venue as additional insured — a separate document from any coverage a caterer or DJ already carries on their own business. Public gardens and historic estates tend to enforce this more consistently than a standard rented hall, precisely because the building or grounds are harder to replace if something goes wrong.

How much coverage do venues typically ask for?

$1 million per occurrence and $2 million aggregate is a common baseline for outdoor and public-venue weddings, though some city- or county-run gardens set a higher figure. The venue’s rental packet should state its own minimum — do not assume a generic figure covers a specific venue’s requirement.

How much does one-day wedding liability insurance cost?

Basic policies commonly run well under $200, and higher-coverage or weather-related add-ons cost more on top of that. It is usually one of the smaller line items in a glasshouse wedding budget, which is part of why it is easy to forget until a venue asks for proof.

How close to the wedding date can I buy event insurance?

Often up until shortly before the event itself, which makes it easy to postpone. That said, buying it once the date and headcount are set removes one more thing to track in the final weeks, and some venues want the certificate on file well before the day.

Do I need a permit as well as insurance?

At public gardens and municipal glasshouses, often yes — a separate use or event permit with its own application, sometimes with a longer lead time than the insurance itself. Ask both questions together when you tour, since one rarely substitutes for the other.

Who is actually responsible for providing proof of insurance — the couple or the vendors?

It depends on the venue and the requirement. A couple’s own liability policy is separate from a caterer’s or florist’s business insurance, and a venue may require either, both, or just one depending on who is doing what on site. Get this in writing rather than assuming it is covered by someone else’s policy.
