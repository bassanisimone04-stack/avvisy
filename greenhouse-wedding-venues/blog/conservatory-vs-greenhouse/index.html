Conservatory vs. greenhouse vs. orangery: a field guide

Six words get used for the same kind of venue search. Here is what each one actually means, and why the difference is worth knowing before you tour.

By GreenhouseVenues editorial

In short

  • The label tells you about the architecture; what decides your day is whether the room is fully enclosed and how it is ventilated.
  • Greenhouse = built for plants. Conservatory = built to be lived in. Orangery = a conservatory with a solid roof. Palm house / atrium = the tall, grand version.
  • A limonaia is the Italian version: a lemon house, built to overwinter citrus.
  • Ask what the room actually is, not just what the venue calls it — the words are used loosely.

Why the label is worth untangling

“Greenhouse wedding venue” is the search term, but it covers at least six genuinely different kinds of building, and couples often tour one expecting the character of another. A working greenhouse and a Victorian orangery are both “glass buildings,” but they behave, heat and price very differently. Knowing the difference before you tour saves a wasted visit.

Greenhouse

A growing structure, built for plants rather than people. Usually the cheapest way to marry under glass, and the most likely to need power, heating, restrooms or floor protection brought in for the day, since none of that was the building’s original job. Expect a dirt or gravel floor in some cases, and ask specifically what infrastructure exists versus what a rental company needs to supply.

Conservatory

A glazed room attached to a building, historically used to overwinter tender plants and, later, to sit in. Finished conservatories usually already have heating, power, flooring and restrooms nearby, which is why they cost more to hire than a working greenhouse but need less brought in.

Orangery

A conservatory’s masonry relative: solid brick or stone piers between tall glazed openings, with a roof that is partly or fully solid rather than all glass. That solid roof traps less summer heat and holds winter heat far better, which is why an orangery is often the glasshouse-adjacent room a garden keeps bookable latest into the year.

Palm house and atrium

A palm house is the tall, domed Victorian type built to hold full-grown palms, usually found in public botanical gardens rather than private estates. An atrium is its modern equivalent — steel and glass, and more often part of a larger building than freestanding. Both tend to be taller and larger than a private conservatory, which changes how the acoustics and the light behave at an event.

Limonaia (and botanical garden glasshouses)

A limonaia is the Italian tradition: a lemon house, built with tall doors and a long glazed wall to overwinter citrus trees in terracotta pots. See our Italy destination collection if that is the direction you are considering. Separately, a botanical garden venue may be any of the types above, wrapped inside a public collection that usually also offers a ceremony lawn — see our cost guide for how that changes pricing.

What actually matters, whatever it is called

  1. Is it fully enclosed, or glass on some sides only? The name does not guarantee full weather coverage.
  2. How is it ventilated? Opening vents, louvers or mechanical cooling — this decides comfort more than the architectural style does.
  3. What is already there versus what needs to be brought in? Power, heat, flooring, restrooms.
  4. What is the glazing? Single-glazed historic glass and modern double glazing hold temperature very differently.

Quick answers

Glasshouse type FAQs

What is the difference between a conservatory and a greenhouse?

A greenhouse is a working growing structure, built for plants first. A conservatory is a glazed room attached to a building, historically for overwintering tender plants, and finished to be lived in — heating, power and flooring already in place. The words get used loosely, so ask what the specific room actually is rather than trusting the name alone.

What is an orangery, and is it different from a conservatory?

An orangery is a conservatory’s masonry cousin: solid brick or stone piers between tall glazed openings, and a roof that is partly or fully solid rather than all glass. That solid roof makes it far easier to heat than a fully glazed room, which is why orangeries are often the venue a garden keeps open latest into the year.

What counts as a palm house or an atrium?

A palm house is the tall, domed Victorian type built to hold full-grown palms, found mostly in public botanical gardens. An atrium is its modern equivalent: steel and glass, usually part of a larger building rather than freestanding. Both tend to be larger and taller than a private-estate conservatory, which shows up in how the light and the acoustics feel.

Does the type of glasshouse affect the price?

Indirectly. Working greenhouses are usually the cheapest to hire and the most likely to need heating, power or floor protection brought in. Finished conservatories and orangeries cost more to hire but include most of that already. See the cost guide for the full breakdown.
