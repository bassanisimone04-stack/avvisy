How many guests actually fit? Capacity planning for glass venues

A venue’s headline capacity figure is rarely the number that matters for your actual layout — here is the math behind it, and the questions to ask instead.

By GreenhouseVenues editorial

Published 2026-09-18 · Updated 2026-09-18

On this page

  1. The rough math
  2. Ceremony vs. reception
  3. Does planting reduce capacity
  4. Booking at the maximum
Browse venues

In short

  • Plan roughly 12–15 sq ft per guest for a seated dinner, plus 2–4 sq ft per guest if there is a dance floor.
  • A venue’s published "maximum capacity" is often a standing-room or fire-code figure, not a seated-dinner figure.
  • Ceremony and reception capacity are usually two different numbers for the same room.
  • This directory only publishes a capacity figure once it is confirmed on the venue’s own page — a dash means unconfirmed, not small.

The rough math

As a general planning figure — not specific to any listed venue — a seated dinner at round tables needs about 12 to 15 square feet per guest, covering the table, chairs and room to move between them. A dance floor adds roughly 2 to 4 square feet per guest on top of that, and a band, DJ booth, bar or photo backdrop each take a bite out of the room before a single guest arrives.

None of this is specific to glasshouses, but it matters more in one: a genuinely beautiful 100-person room can turn out to seat 70 once a dance floor, bar and planting are accounted for.

Why ceremony and reception capacity differ

A ceremony layout — rows of chairs facing one point — uses floor space far more efficiently than a reception layout with round tables and a dance floor. The same room can genuinely hold close to twice as many people for a ceremony as it can for a seated dinner. Ask a venue for both figures specifically; a single "capacity" number on a listing page is almost always one or the other, not both.

Does planting reduce usable capacity?

Often, yes, and it is easy to miss. A working greenhouse’s listed square footage does not always account for permanent benches, raised beds or large potted specimens that stay in place during an event. Ask whether a quoted capacity already assumes the room as it normally looks, or an empty shell.

Should you book right at the maximum?

Generally not. A common rule of thumb among planners is to target around 80% of a venue’s maximum for a comfortable guest experience, leaving room for walkways, a gift table, accessibility needs and vendor space. A room that is technically large enough on paper can still feel genuinely packed at full capacity — see our venue checklist for the rest of the questions worth asking on a tour.

Quick answers

Capacity planning FAQs

How many guests actually fit in a glasshouse or conservatory?

As a rough planning figure, a seated dinner needs about 12–15 square feet per guest, and a dance floor adds roughly 2–4 square feet per guest on top of that. A venue’s published “maximum capacity” is often a standing-room or fire-code figure, which can overstate what comfortably fits with a full seated reception and dancing.

Why does a venue give a different capacity for a ceremony versus a reception?

A ceremony layout (rows of chairs facing one point) uses floor space far more efficiently than a reception layout (round tables, a dance floor, a bar, a band). The same room can honestly hold twice as many people for a ceremony as it can for a seated dinner — ask for both figures, not just one.

Does planting inside a working greenhouse reduce usable capacity?

Often, yes — a listed square footage does not always subtract the floor space that permanent benches, raised beds or large potted specimens actually occupy. Ask whether the quoted capacity already accounts for the planting in place, or assumes an empty room.

Should I book a venue at its maximum listed capacity?

Generally no — most planners suggest targeting around 80% of a venue’s maximum for a comfortable guest experience, leaving room for walkways, a gift table, accessibility needs and vendor space. A venue that is technically large enough can still feel packed at full capacity.

Why does this directory leave capacity blank for some listings?

Because we only publish a capacity figure once we can confirm it on the venue’s own page — see how we source listings. A dash means unconfirmed, not small.

Can a glasshouse wedding work for 200 or more guests?

Yes — while much of this directory’s content leans toward small and micro weddings, larger conservatories and purpose-built glass event spaces do exist that seat 200 to 300 or more. They are less common than smaller rooms, so expect fewer options and a longer search, not a smaller venue category.

Does a large glasshouse wedding need anything a small one does not?

Usually more of everything a smaller wedding already has to think about: ventilation capacity, electrical supply for more vendors, a wider load-in route, and often a stricter minimum-spend or exclusivity requirement. See our venue checklist for the full list of questions, which apply at any size.
